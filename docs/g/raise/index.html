Ask for a raise and get it

Use real pay data and Claude to build your case, practice the conversation, and know exactly what to say when they push back.

TIMEAbout 45 minutes
YOU NEEDYour job title, location, a list of your wins, and Claude
COSTFree
Most raises go to people who ask with a clear case: what the market pays, what you've delivered, and a specific number. Claude helps you build all three and rehearse until it feels easy.

Step by step

1

Get real pay data

Look up your job on the Bureau of Labor Statistics wage data. It shows pay ranges (10th to 90th percentile) by job and by city. Then compare job posts for your role: in several states, including California, Colorado, New York and Washington, job ads must include a pay range.

BLS wage data by job and area ↗BLS occupation search ↗
2

Turn your work into proof

Dump everything you did this year into Claude, messy is fine:

Here's everything I did at work this year: [paste notes, projects, emails, praise, numbers]. Turn it into 5 to 7 clear wins. For each one: what I did, the result, and the number that proves it (money saved or made, time saved, customers, growth). Ask me for any number you think is missing. Then rank them by what my manager cares about most.
3

Pick your number

Give Claude your data and let it build the case:

My role: [title] in [city]. My current pay: $[amount]. Market data I found: [BLS percentiles and job post ranges]. My wins: [paste]. Recommend a specific raise to ask for, with a reason it's fair, a number slightly above my target, and the lowest I should accept. Explain it in 3 sentences I can say out loud.
4

Write the ask and rehearse it

Ask for a meeting on its own, not inside a regular check in. Then practice:

Write a short message asking my manager for a 30 minute meeting to talk about my compensation. Then play my manager in a practice conversation. Push back realistically: no budget, wait until review season, you're paid fairly, what about a title instead. After each of my answers, coach me on what to say better. Keep it natural, not scripted.
5

If the answer is not now

Don't leave empty handed. Ask what exactly you need to do to get there, and by when, and get it in writing. Have Claude write the follow up email the same day.

Write a short follow up email to my manager after our pay conversation. Thank them, summarize what we agreed: [what they said], the goals I need to hit, and the date we'll revisit. Keep it positive and professional.

Watch out

  • Pay data is a starting point. Your company, industry and experience change the number.
  • Never share confidential company information with any AI tool. Describe your wins in general terms if needed.
  • Be honest. Don't claim a competing offer you don't have.
