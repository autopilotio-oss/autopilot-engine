Get your money back

Canceled flights, double charges, things that never arrived. You often have a legal right to a refund. Here's how to claim it with Claude.

Companies don't always refund you on their own. A short written request that names the rule is easy for them to approve, and it starts the paper trail you need if you have to escalate.

Step by step

1

2

Flights: know the rule

Under U.S. Department of Transportation rules, if an airline cancels your flight for any reason, or makes a significant change, and you turn down the alternatives (a new flight, a credit or a voucher), you're owed your money back, not just a voucher. Significant means departing or arriving 3+ hours off on a domestic trip or 6+ hours off on an international one, a different airport, extra connections, or a downgrade to a lower cabin. If you bought from the airline, the refund should be automatic once you decline. Card refunds are due within 7 business days, other payment methods take longer. If you take the changed flight, you generally can't get the ticket refunded.

3

Ask the company, but watch the clock

For a card charge, your 60 day dispute window (step 4) keeps running while you wait on the company. The FTC suggests telling your card company early so you don't miss it. Then paste your booking or receipt details and use this:

Write a refund request to [company]. Order or booking number: [number]. What happened: [facts and dates]. What I want: a refund of $[amount] to my original payment method, not a credit or voucher. If it applies, mention my rights [for flights: the DOT refund rule for canceled or significantly changed flights / for card purchases: my right to dispute billing errors with my card issuer]. Keep it short, specific and polite, list what I'm attaching, and ask for a reply within 7 days.
4

Credit card: dispute it in writing

If the company won't fix a double charge, a charge for something you returned, or an order that never arrived as agreed, call your card company, then send a written billing error notice. Federal law gives you this right, but only if the letter reaches the issuer within 60 days after the first statement with the charge was sent to you. Mail it to the billing inquiries address on your statement, not the payment address. Certified mail with a return receipt gives you proof. The issuer must confirm in writing within 30 days and resolve it within 90. You can hold off paying the disputed amount while they investigate; keep paying the rest.

Write a billing error letter to my credit card issuer under the Fair Credit Billing Act. My name and address: [ ]. Card: ending in [last 4]. The charge: [merchant, date, amount], first shown on my statement dated [date]. What is wrong: [billed twice / returned on (date) / never delivered / not as agreed]. What I did with the merchant: [dates and their answers]. Ask them to remove the charge and any related fees and to confirm in writing. List the copies I'm enclosing (never originals). Plain and polite, under one page. Then tell me the date 60 days after my statement date so I know my deadline.

5

Escalate if they say no

For airlines, file a complaint with DOT. For a bank or card company that won't handle your dispute properly, you can submit a complaint to the CFPB. Have Claude turn your notes into a short, dated timeline first; complaints with clear dates are easier to act on:

Turn these notes into a dated timeline for a complaint: [paste emails, dates, reference numbers]. One line per event: date, who, what happened. Then write a 5 sentence complaint summary and the outcome I'm asking for. Facts only, no guesses.

6

Keep a paper trail

Save every email, screenshot, reference number and the certified mail receipt in one folder. Paste new replies into the same Claude chat so the timeline stays current.

Watch out

  • Claude helps you write the request. The company, the DOT or your card issuer decides the outcome.
  • Only dispute charges that are actually wrong. A dispute isn't a way to get out of a purchase you agreed to.
  • Debit cards work differently. For a debit or bank charge you didn't authorize, tell your bank within 60 days of the statement it appeared on; waiting longer can leave you paying for it. For other debit problems, ask your bank how it handles disputes.
  • Phone calls don't protect your credit card dispute rights on their own. Send the written notice too, and keep a copy.
